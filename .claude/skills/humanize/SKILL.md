# Humanize: write prose without AI tells

Apply these rules to any prose you write or edit. When rewriting existing text, fix violations without changing meaning. The goal is writing that reads like a specific person wrote it: concrete, plain, unpadded.

## Banned vocabulary

Never use (in prose): delve, crucial, pivotal, vital, robust, vibrant, tapestry, landscape (abstract), testament, underscore (verb), highlight (verb, figurative), showcase, boasts, bolstered, fostering, garner, meticulous, intricate, enduring, seamless, comprehensive, leverage (verb), elevate, empower, unlock, harness, "key" as adjective, "align with", "Additionally" at sentence start, "valuable insights", "diverse array", "rich heritage", "nestled", "in the heart of", groundbreaking, renowned, "evolving landscape", "focal point", "indelible mark".

If a banned word is the literal technical term (e.g., `underscore` the character, a robust statistical estimator), it's fine.

## Banned sentence patterns

- **Significance inflation**: "marks a pivotal moment", "plays a vital role", "reflects broader trends", "stands as a testament", "setting the stage for", "cementing its legacy", "underscores its importance". State the fact; don't announce that it matters.
- **Trailing -ing analysis clauses**: "..., highlighting its importance", "..., ensuring reliability", "..., reflecting a commitment to X". End the sentence at the fact.
- **Negative parallelisms**: "not just X, but Y", "it's not X — it's Y", "no X, no Y, just Z", formulaic "X rather than Y".
- **Rule of three**: reflexive triplets ("fast, simple, and reliable"). Use two items, or four, or one — whatever is true.
- **Copula avoidance**: write "is", "has", "was" — not "serves as", "stands as", "functions as", "features", "offers", "boasts", "refers to".
- **Vague attribution**: "experts argue", "observers note", "widely regarded", "industry reports suggest". Name the source or drop the claim.
- **Formulaic wrap-ups**: "In summary", "In conclusion", "Overall,", "Despite these challenges, X continues to...". End when the content ends.
- **Hedging disclaimers**: "it's important to note", "it's worth noting", "keep in mind".
- **Elegant variation**: repeating a word is fine; cycling through strained synonyms ("the tool... the utility... the solution... the platform") is a tell.

## Banned formatting habits

- Em dashes as punchy emphasis, especially spaced ( — ). One per paragraph at most; prefer commas, colons, or parentheses.
- Bold inline-header bullet lists ("**Speed**: it's fast"). Use prose or plain lists.
- Boldface for emphasis scattered through prose.
- Emoji as section decoration or bullet markers.
- Title Case Headings — use sentence case.
- Small tables for facts that fit in a sentence.
- Curly quotes in code-adjacent or plain-text contexts.
- A heading + subheading skeleton for content that is three paragraphs long.

## Prefer instead

- Simple verbs: used (not utilized), wrote (not authored), tried (not attempted), moved (not relocated), died (not passed away), has (not is equipped with).
- Concrete specifics over generic praise: "handles 40k requests/min on one node" beats "highly performant and scalable".
- Superlatives only when literally true and checkable.
- Varied sentence length, including short ones.
- Plain constructions humans actually use: "there is a", "it has a", "as a result of", "in order to".
- Letting facts sit without a closing line about what they demonstrate.

## Self-check before finishing

Reread the output once. If any sentence could be pasted into a press release about a different product without edits, it's too generic — replace it with a specific fact or cut it.
